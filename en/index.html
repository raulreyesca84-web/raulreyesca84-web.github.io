Academic profile

Philosophy, Historicity, and Transformation

I hold a Doctorate in Humanities, specializing in Moral and Political Philosophy, from Universidad Autónoma Metropolitana–Iztapalapa, where I received the Medalla al Mérito Universitario. I also hold a Master’s degree and a Bachelor’s degree in Philosophy from Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, both awarded with honors.

My philosophical work is organized around a fundamental question: how are forms of oppression and domination historically constituted, legitimized, and transformed, and what concepts have philosophers developed to understand their reproduction, their contradictions, and the ruptures that may emerge within them?

This question runs from the Reformation and early modern debates on liberty, property, and slavery to the critique of capitalism, the political event, and contemporary transformations of the state under financialization.

Research program

Philosophy of Subversion

I use the term Philosophy of Subversion for the research program that brings together my work on the historical transformations of capitalism, the constitution of orders of domination, and the philosophical categories through which their crises and ruptures have been thought.

“Subversion” does not designate, within this program, a political doctrine or a predetermined historical project. It names a philosophical problem: how and under what conditions an order that presents and reproduces itself as stable can enter into contradiction, be challenged, and give rise to new forms of subjectivation, legitimacy, and organization of common life.

The program does not begin from a simple opposition between dominators and dominated. It reconstructs the economic, legal, political, religious, cultural, and subjective mediations through which an order acquires consistency, while also examining the contradictions within universal discourses of liberty, right, and humanity.

01

Historicity

Political concepts change because the social relations they seek to understand also change.

02

Contradiction

Liberty, property, right, and universality can contain both emancipatory potentials and forms of exclusion.

03

Totality

Economy, politics, law, religion, and subjectivity must be reconstructed through their mediations rather than understood as isolated phenomena.

Subversion

Rupture is itself an object of inquiry: the question is how it emerges, how it has been thought, and what new forms of subjectivation it opens.

Historical object

Capitalism: Production, Reproduction, and the Transformation of Social Life

I understand capitalism as a mode of production and reproduction that has profoundly transformed political, social, economic, and cultural life. Its logic is not exhausted by the market: it reshapes institutions, property relations, forms of subjectivity, and conditions of social interaction.

One of its structural fault lines lies between those who own and control capital—productive or financial—and those who depend upon economic relations they do not control. This asymmetry does not arise from an original condition of material equality: it is traversed by historical processes of appropriation, accumulation, dispossession, colonialism, and slavery.

Capitalism produces contradictions—concentrations of wealth, inequality, exclusion, and social conflict—that it may absorb, displace, or manage without necessarily resolving them. This is why it is necessary to reconstruct historically the changing forms assumed by these contradictions.

Methodological hypothesis

Dialectic of Contingency and Subversion

The Dialectic of Contingency and Subversion reconstructs the mediations through which a historical totality takes shape in particular forms of domination, examines its contradictions, and studies the ruptures through which an order may be challenged or transformed without those transformations being mechanically determined by the conditions that precede them.

Totality, Mediation, and Determination

This procedure draws on a central lesson from Hegel, Marx, and Lukács: concepts do not remain fixed but acquire increasingly concrete determinations as they are confronted with their relations, limits, and contradictions. From Hegel comes the requirement to follow the development of determinations; from Marx, the need to confront them with the material conditions of social life; and from Lukács, the movement between totality, mediation, and particularity. Philosophy is thus not reduced to a succession of doctrines: it is interrogated through what it sought to think in its own historical moment.

Liberty and Slavery

Slavery constitutes, in this sense, a crucial test for philosophies of freedom. A universal concept of freedom acquires critical force when it is capable of confronting concrete historical forms of its negation, even when the philosopher who formulates it remains constrained by the prejudices or limitations of his own time. Philosophy can reproduce its historical moment, but it can also develop concepts capable of challenging some of its forms of domination.

Transposition, Distancing, Torsion, and Obliquity

This dialectic is not confined to a single philosophical tradition. It can move through other philosophies, distance itself from their limitations, appropriate their resources, and subject them to operations of transposition, distancing, torsion, and obliquity. In my reading of Marx, these operations help explain why his relation to Hegel cannot be reduced to a simple inversion. Marx rejects certain claims of speculative philosophy while appropriating dialectic, displacing it toward material conditions, and transforming it into a critical instrument for thinking historical contradictions. Thought can therefore learn from other philosophies without becoming captive to any one of them.

Contingency and Event

No structure, however, allows its own transformation to be completely deduced. Contradictions prepare conditions, but they do not guarantee rupture. This is where contingency enters. Events, revolutions, and processes of subjectivation do not emerge from nothing: they are preceded by material relations, institutions, conflicts, and political languages. Yet neither can they be mechanically reduced to those conditions. Theories of the event— particularly the work of Alain Badiou—make it possible to think this emergence of novelty and to test how far a structure can explain that which interrupts it.

Subversion

Subversion constitutes the other pole of the method. A philosophy of forms of domination remains incomplete if it cannot also think the ways in which an order may be challenged, interrupted, or transformed. Such ruptures do not possess an emancipatory meaning in advance: their orientations and consequences must be examined historically. Emancipation is one of their possibilities, not their necessary outcome.

Historical and Contemporary Projection

This movement makes it possible to study both philosophical responses to slavery and contemporary transformations of the state under neoliberal financialization. In the latter case, I am particularly interested in the figure of a permeable state: formally sovereign, yet traversed by private economic, financial, and legal powers capable of constraining its effective capacity for decision. The research thus moves between totality and particularity, structure and event, reproduction and rupture, in order to understand how forms of domination are historically constituted and under what conditions they may be contested and transformed.

Reflective turning point

Human Dignity as a Reflective Horizon

Human dignity constitutes a reflective turning point within this program. I cannot conceive it philosophically apart from freedom, substantive equality, and justice.

These categories do not designate a predetermined political program, nor do they prescribe a specific form of state, economy, or collective action. They constitute problems from which philosophy can interrogate historical forms of human life, examine their contradictions, and ask under what conditions dignity is recognized, restricted, or denied.

Freedom occupies a central place because it makes it possible to confront universal concepts with their concrete historical realizations. Slavery constitutes one of its most extreme negations; other configurations of property, dependency, inequality, or power allow us to ask, in different contexts, how far a society actually realizes what it proclaims.

Substantive equality and justice broaden this inquiry without predetermining its answer. The philosophical task consists in keeping this horizon of reflection open: thinking forms of domination, the contradictions of historical orders, and the contingent possibilities through which they may be challenged or transformed.

Research map

Architecture of the Philosophy of Subversion

The program is organized into interconnected research nuclei linked by common problems: the historical constitution of orders of domination, the contradictions of liberty, the mediations between economy, law, and politics, and the contingent conditions of their transformation. These nuclei do not constitute closed stages. They are lines of inquiry that develop and reinterpret one another.

I

Reformation, Authority, and Rupture

Luther and Müntzer make it possible to study a religious transformation that reorganized relations among conscience, authority, and political order. In Müntzer, rupture also acquires a theological-political dimension that allows us to interrogate the possibilities of challenging a historical order.

Related work: Martín Lutero: la Reforma protestante y los orígenes oscuros de la modernidad; “La teología política de Thomas Müntzer”.

II

Liberty, Property, and Slavery

Locke, Leibniz, and Rousseau make it possible to reconstruct the internal tensions of early modern philosophy among liberty, property, natural right, and slavery. Universal principles of liberty can historically coexist with exclusions and forms of domination; at the same time, some of those concepts can provide resources for challenging them.

Related work: Locke, colonialism, and slavery; Leibniz, slavery and race; Rousseau, property, liberty, and slavery.

III

Capitalism, the Liberal State, and Slavery

This nucleus investigates the relationship between capitalist transformation and legal-political institutionalization. The expansion of new relations of property, production, and commerce requires institutional forms capable of securing them, yet these transformations are historically asymmetric and may coexist with relations of slavery. The problem makes it possible to connect the history of early modern philosophy with research into subsequent transformations of the state and capitalism.

Current research: Capitalism, the Liberal State, and Slavery.

IV

Freedom, Institutions, and Totality

Hegel makes it possible to think freedom as something that must become actual in law and institutions. Lukács extends the problem through the category of totality: economy, politics, culture, law, and subjectivity cannot be understood as isolated domains but through their historical relations and mediations.

Related work: Hegel and the idea of freedom; Lukács and Hegelian dialectic.

V

Capital, Event, and Subject

Marx shifts the inquiry toward the material conditions and historical contradictions of capitalism. Althusser and Badiou make it possible to formulate a complementary problem: how can philosophy think ruptures that cannot be mechanically deduced from a structure, and how is the subject capable of sustaining the consequences of an event constituted?

Related work: Marx’s antiphilosophy; Althusser and Badiou; political event and subversion.

VI

Neoliberalism, Financialization, and the Transformation of Political Forms

The contemporary line of research examines how transformations of capitalism reshape the capacities of the state, sovereignty, rights, and the relations between public and private power. From this perspective, I also examine political forms that emerge or are reorganized under the historical conditions of neoliberalism and financialization.

Related work: “La degradación del Estado y del sujeto en la ideología neoliberal”; ongoing research on neoliberalism, neopopulism, and neofascism.

Current research

Capitalism, the State, and Historical Forms of Domination

Capitalism, the Liberal State, and Slavery

One of my current research projects examines the relationship between capitalist transformation and the constitution of new legal-political forms. The working hypothesis holds that transformations in economic relations require processes of institutionalization capable of securing new forms of property, production, circulation, and commerce.

This transformation should not be understood as a homogeneous or simultaneous transition. Economic, legal, and political institutions change at different rates and may preserve relations inherited from earlier historical configurations. Slavery provides a particularly revealing test of this articulation: its persistence within transforming economies requires us to interrogate the relations among liberty, property, labor, commerce, and the constitution of the liberal state.

This line of inquiry extends my research on Locke, Leibniz, and Rousseau, while shifting the question from the internal consistency of their concepts toward the historical and institutional conditions under which liberty, property, and slavery could coexist.

State, Sovereignty, and Financialization

A second line of research examines contemporary transformations in the relationship between capitalism and the state. The mobility of capital, transnational legal architectures, and private networks of intermediation raise questions about the effective capacity of states to regulate economic processes that cross their borders and condition public decision-making.

Of particular interest here is the tension between formal sovereignty and effective capacity for intervention. This research extends the problem of the mediations among economy, law, and politics and makes it possible to examine how forms of power, dependency, and domination change historically.
